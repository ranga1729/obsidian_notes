#### Initial Dilemma of AI
the initial dilemma of AI was introduced by Alan Turing in his paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence” published in 1950. 
Main focus: creating machines with human like thinking and reasoning abilities, and bridging the gap between human intelligence and computational systems

Turing Paradigm: Evaluating a machine’s ability to show human intelligence indistinguishable from a human.

Machines are considered to be intelligent if and only if it can convince humans that is is not a machine. And the responses provided by the AI artifact are expected to be identical to the ordinary behavior of a human. 

In addition to the Turing Paradigm, several other AI paradigms were suggested as a result of the initial dilemma of AI,
1. Turing Paradigm
2. Connectionist Paradigm
3. Evolutionary Paradigm
4. Bayesian Paradigm
5. Fuzzy Logic Paradigm

#### 1. Turing Paradigm
The Turing Paradigm (often associated with Symbolic AI or the "Physical Symbol System Hypothesis") views intelligence as the manipulation of explicit symbols according to formal syntactic rules.

Rooted in Alan Turing’s concept of universal computation, this paradigm posits that intelligence can be fully realized by a system that processes discrete symbolic representations (like words, numbers, or logic tokens). Knowledge is explicitly programmed into the system using formal logic, semantic networks, or production rules, and reasoning is achieved through inference engines. This was the dominant approach during the "Good Old-Fashioned AI" (GOFAI) era, focusing on top-down logic rather than bottom-up data learning.
#### 2. Connectionist Paradigm
Focuses on the idea of modeling the structure and functions of the brain to develop artificial neural networks. 

Instead of relying on explicit, human-coded rules, knowledge in a connectionist system is distributed across the network(Neural Network) and stored in the weights of the connections between units. Learning occurs through data-driven optimization algorithms (such as backpropagation) that iteratively adjust these weights to minimize an error function. This paradigm forms the foundational basis for modern **Deep Learning** and artificial neural networks.
#### 3. Evolutionary Paradigm
Focuses on using evolutionary algorithms, concepts of natural selection, and genetic mutation to optimize solutions to problems. 

It utilizes **Genetic Algorithms (GAs)** and evolutionary programming to search a vast space of potential solutions. A population of candidate solutions (represented as chromosomes) undergoes iterative generations of evaluation via a fitness function. The highest-performing solutions are selected to undergo stochastic operations like **crossover** (recombination of attributes) and **mutation** (random alterations) to produce the next generation. This approach is highly effective for complex optimization problems where the search space is poorly understood or discontinuous.

#### 4. Bayesian Paradigm
Focuses on identifying relationships between different variables and calculating probabilities of different outcomes.

Instead of outputting deterministic binary conclusions, Bayesian systems maintain and update a probability distribution over a set of possible states as new data is observed. This paradigm is fundamental to machine learning algorithms like Naive Bayes classifiers, Bayesian networks, and Gaussian processes.

#### 5. Fuzzy Logic Paradigm
Focuses on providing reasoning similar to humans in situations where there can be multiple in-between values for the possibilities rather than just true or false.

In fuzzy logic, variables possess a **degree of truth** mapped to a continuous scale between $0$ and $1$ via membership functions. This allows AI systems to mathematically model human-like concepts (e.g., classifying a temperature not just as "hot" or "cold," but as "75% warm"). The system processes these inputs using fuzzy rules (IF-THEN statements with fuzzy sets) and defuzzifies the output into a crisp, actionable control signal. It is widely used in expert systems and automated control systems.

### Software-based AI Applications
##### 1. Natural Language Processing(NLP)
NLP “teaches” computers to understand, interpret, and generate natural language in a way humans communicate with each other.
It's sub-domain,
- Natural Language Understanding
- Machine Translations
- Language Generation
- Speech Understanding

Ex: Google Translator, GPT-3, Grammarly 

##### 2. Computer Vision
It teaches computers to "see" like humans and be able to analyze, interpret, and understand images and videos.
Ex: 
	Google photos, 
	FaceID apps
	Medical Imaging systems such as IBM Watson Health has the ability to analyze medical images to make more accurate diagnoses
	Snapchat filters uses computer vision along with Augmented Reality concepts to apply certain effects on users’ faces in real-time

##### 3. Speech Recognition Systems
Have the ability to convert spoken languages into text or commands that a computer can understand.
Ex:
	Smart home appliances 
	Google Assistant
	Apple's Siri
	Amazon Alexa

##### 4. Expert Systems
An **expert system** is a specialized computer program that emulates the decision-making ability of a human expert in a specific domain. Instead of relying on data-driven machine learning, these artificial intelligence (AI) systems use a pre-programmed "knowledge base" and logical rules to solve complex problems. 

Ex: 
	Financial decision making systems
	Classification systems
	Diagnosis systems
	Recommendation systems

##### 2. Autonomous Vehicles
Self-driving (autonomous) vehicles use Artificial Intelligence fed with sensor data and camera captures to travel between destinations without human operation or with minimum human intervention.

AI algorithms are increasingly being used in drones to help them navigate and make decisions in real-time. This requires specialized hardware that is small and lightweight, yet powerful enough to run the necessary algorithms.

Several companies, including Google, Intel, and NVIDIA, have developed specialized chips designed for AI tasks. These chips can perform the matrix multiplication operations needed for deep learning algorithms more efficiently than traditional CPUs and GPUs.
