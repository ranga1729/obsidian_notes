### Criminal Law and Civil Law
##### 1. Criminal Law
Criminal law represents the society's view of the minimum standard of acceptable behavior. 
It defines,
1. What constitutes a crime ?
2. Mechanisms for deciding whether a person accused of a crime is guilty or innocent
3. Range of punishments applicable to different categories of crime
Police are responsible for discovering who has carried out a specific criminal offence and for collecting evidence.
Criminal court will prosecute the persons in concerned(accused or defendant).
##### 2. Civil Law
Purpose of the civil law is to provide rules for settling dispute between people. 

> [!NOTE] Legal Person
> A **"legal person"** is an organization that has gone through a process called incorporation that give it the same legal status as a natural person.

Several ways of an organization can be incorporated,
1. Act of parliament
2. Registering as a company
3. The grant of royal charter
 This allows companies/organizations to be identified as a legal person at the court. 

Court action under the civil law is known as **litigation**.
It must be initiated by one of the parties to the dispute.
The person who initiates the court action is known as the **claimant**(or plaintiff).
###### Standard of proof and Burden of proof
- For a person to be found guilty of a criminal offence, the prosecution must demonstrate that he or she is ==guilty beyond all reasonable doubt==. 
- The standard of proof required in criminal cases is higher than it required in civil cases.
- In a criminal case, the ==burden of proof lies on the prosecution==(Prosecution has to prove its case). ==Defendant do not need to prove their innocence. They are assumed to be innocent until they are proven guilty==. 
- In civil cases, both parties present their arguments and must convince the court of their correctness. 
### Origin or Law
England and Wales has two main sources of law,
1. Common law
2. Statue law
##### Common law
Common law is the traditional law that is not written down but which depends on the judgment of judges over the centuries. When deciding the rights and wrongs of a case, a court will look at the way in which similar cases have been decided in the past. Those cases are called **"precedents"**.
- This common law tradition is shared by many other countries(all most all the commonwealth countries, USA, Singapore...)
- Europe countries doesn't use the common law tradition. (Ex: France, Germany)
##### Statue law
Laid down by acts of parliament. Often referred to as legislation. 
Ex: Child labour laws, Laws of misuse of computers
In some areas, the millions of common law judgements from the past make it difficult for courts to apply the common law. So the parliament has passed a legislation to bring together the common law in these areas into a single statue. 
Ex: Theft Act 1968, which consolidated the common law provisions regarding crimes involving stealing. 
### The legislative process in the UK
UK has the **two-chamber(bicameral legislature)** system. 
This means the law-making(parliament) body is made up of two chambers or groups of people. 
1. House of Common
2. House of Lords
##### House of Common
Members of parliament are elected and everyone over the age of 18 has a vote. 
UK is divided into 659 constituencies, each of which elects(most votes) one member of parliament. This is called the **"first past the post"** system.
##### House of Lords
Most of its members are appointed but significant number are chosen from amongst the hereditary peers. 
(Hereditary peers are members of the aristocracy who inherit their titles and seats in the House of Lords. These titles are passed down through families, typically from father to son, and the right to sit in the House of Lords was traditionally linked to these inherited peerages.)
This practice has been significantly reduced in recent times.

##### British Government
![[slide-1 1.jpg]]
British government is made up of members from both the House of Common and the House of Lords. 
Members from the House of Lords are never more than a small proportion.
Members from the House of common are always as the Prime Minister, the Chancellor of the Exchequer, the foreign secretary and the Home secretary. 

Most new legislations re initiated by the government. They are introduced in the form of a **bill**, a set of proposals that parliament is invited to discuss. 

Process of creating a law,
1. New bills are usually introduced first in the House of Commons. 
2. It will be discussed and possibly amended.
3. Then approves by the House of Common. 
4. Then the bill is passes to the House of Lords. 
5. If the house of lords approved the bill, it becomes an **"Act of Parliament"**.
	If the House or Lords rejects a bill or modifies it, the bill is returned to the House of Commons for further considerations. 
6. It is then passed to the Queen for her formal approval.(the royal assent). Then it becomes a law. 
	The Queen cannot refuse approve when parliament has approved a bill. 

Acts of parliament are usually referred to by their title, followed by the year in which they received the royal assent. 
Ex: Computer misuse Act 1990

The House of Common has the power to override any changes that may have been made by the house of lords or even to insist that a bill rejected by the house of lords should/nevertheless be passed to the royal assent. 

In addition to the British national parliament, there are separate elected assemblies in Wales and Northern Ireland and a Scottish parliament. they have certain limited powers. 
##### Green Papers
When the government wants to canvass opinion before asking Parliament to approve legislation, it publish a Green paper. 
Green papers explains why the government wants to create new laws and discusses a number of possible approaches. 
The green paper will be discussed by the parliament and comments on it will be invited from the public and interested bodies. 
Ex: BCS(British Computer Society) was specifically asked for their opinion when the Computer Misuse Act was discussed. 
##### White Papers
Once the government has decided its general approach, it publishes a White paper. 
It describes the proposed legislation and it will be used as the basis for discussing the details. 
At the end, government will produce a bill. 
##### Secondary Legislation
Acts of parliament constitute are called **"Primary Legislation"**.
It's impractical to parliament to go through all laws in detail. 
As a solution, Secondary legislation are introduced.

It's the process of introducing detailed legislation without full discussion in parliament. The proposed regulations are placed in the library of the house of commons so that members of either house can look at them. If no objections are raised within a fixed time period , the regulation become law. 
Ex: Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 to protect the designs of semi-conductor chips. 
### The European Union(EU)
The UK is a member of the EU. 
EU is consist of 28 countries. 
The EU has its own parliament elected by individual voters in all the member countries. The EU is run by a commission that has the power to issue directives  that require member countries to modify their own legislations to meet a common standard, if necessary.
These directives must be discussed by the European Parliament and approved by the member states before they come into effect. 
Ex: Data protection and the protection of software. 
### The legislative process in other countries(US)
![[Pasted image 20250817005148.png]]
UK legislature is the Parliament while US legislature is known as **"Congress"**.
Congress consist of two houses,
1. The Senate
2. The House of Representatives
Both houses are elected but with different terms.
##### 1. The House of Representatives
Members(representatives) are elected for a period of ==2 years==. 
Each congressman represents a district and each district contains the (roughly)same number of people. 
##### 2. The Senate
Contains two members(senators) for each state.
Senators are elected for ==7 years==. 

Legislations must be approved by the Senate and the House of Representatives before it becomes a law. 
Neither chambers can override the others.
Then the President must also give his assent before and Act of Congress becomes law. 
President is allowed to veto legislation passed by Congress. 

Supreme court can strike out legislation approved by Congress and the President if it's unconstitutional. 

But in UK, the doctrine of the **sovereignty of Parliament** means that the courts cannot override primary legislation made by Parliament, although they can override secondary legislation. 
##### US Government
Members of the congress are not the members of the government. 
The members of the government are individuals chosen by the President and their appointment must be approved by Congress. 

The founders of the United States wanted to separate these three functions,
1. The legislature, Congress, makes laws.
2. The judiciary, that is the judges and other legal officials, applies these laws in particular cases.
3. The executive, that is the president and the other members of the government, carries on the business of government.

US is a federation of 50 states, each has it's own legislature. 
The **"Issue of states' right"** is the extent to which federal law can override laws made by individual states. 

Smaller countries often have a **"Unicameral"** legislature, that is a parliament that consist of a single chamber. 
Ex: Singapore, Sri Lanka

##### The Law Across Borders
Most countries courts have the power go beyond the border.
Ex: UK can enable criminal charges against their citizens who have engaged in sexual activity with underage partners in foreign countries. 

**Extradition** : Bringing a person from one country to another to face trail for an alleged offence. 

In cases of jurisdiction in Civil cases that across border, the claimant can decide in which country to initiate action.
