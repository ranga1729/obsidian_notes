#### 3. Release Planning
Involves planning, scheduling, and controlling the process of delivering software updates or releases to users.

Different ways of releasing,
- Release after multiple sprint
- Release every sprint
- Release every feature

The inputs to release planning include outputs from product planning,
- The product vision
- High-level product backlog
- Product roadmap

Activities,
- Confirm the release scope, date and budget
- Product backlog grooming
	Create, estimating and prioritizing backlog items. 
- Always review the MRFs
	MRF : Minimum Releasable Feature
- Some organizations create a sprint map during this time

#### Fixed-Date Release Planning
Many organization prefer to user fixed-date releases,
1. Calculate the number of sprints needed to perform in this release.
2. Determine how much work the team can get done in that number of sprints.
3. Determine what features can be completed during the sprints.

Steps to perform a fixed-date release planning,
1. Determine how many sprints are in this release
2. Groom the product backlog. 
3. Measure or estimate the team’s velocity. 
4. Multiply the slower velocity by the number of sprints (will-have line). 
5. Multiply the faster velocity by the number of sprints (might-have line).

#### Fixed-Scope Release planning
1. Calculate the number of sprint needed to perform in this release.
2. determine how much work the team can get done in that number of sprints.

Steps for performing fixed-scope release planning,
1. Groom the product backlog 
2. Determine the total size of the PBI(Product Backlog Item)s 
3. Estimate the team’s velocity as a range 
4. Divide the total size of the PBIs by the faster velocity (round up) 
5. Divide the total size of the PBIs by the slower velocity (round up)

**Calculating Cost**
Identify the team. Determine the sprint length. Based on team composition and sprint length, determine the personnel costs of running a sprint.

For a fixed-date release
	number of sprints x cost per sprint
For a fixed-scoped release
	high and low number of sprints x cost per sprint

**Communicating Progress**
1. Fixed-scope release burn down chart
Shows how much work **remains** over time. The chart "burns down" to zero as tasks are completed. It's useful for tracking whether the team is on pace to finish by the deadline.

2. Fixed-scope release burn up chart
Shows how much work has been **completed** over time, often with a line indicating the total scope. It helps visualize both progress and scope changes.
Similar to the burndown chart, Three predictive lines indicating the likely number of sprints to achieve the target.

3. Fixed-date release
We have to know the number of sprints in the release. 
Goal is to **communicate the range of features we expect to complete** and sprint by sprint progress.
**Burndown and burn-up charts** are **not always suitable for fixed-date releases** because:
	1. These charts are good for tracking progress, but they don’t reliably predict if a team will meet a fixed deadline.
	2. Burndown charts assume that the scope stays constant, but fixed-date projects often face last-minute additions or changes.
	3. These charts track effort (like story points), not necessarily deliverable features or business-critical outcomes, which are essential for fixed-date releases.
	4. For fixed-date releases, understanding the critical path and dependencies is more important than just knowing the rate of progress.
For fixed-date projects, **Gantt charts**, **critical path analysis**, or **release burnup charts with milestone tracking** are often more effective.
